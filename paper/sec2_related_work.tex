\section{Comprehensive State of the Art \& Taxonomic Review (2016--2026)}
\label{sec:sota}

The intersection of adversarial machine learning (AML), network security, and decision tree verification has undergone intensive development over the past decade. To contextualize the theoretical and empirical contributions of Flow-Field Evasion (FFE), this section presents an exhaustive taxonomic analysis of existing paradigms, tracing their evolution from early unconstrained attacks through problem-space formalisms, exact formal verification solvers, and modern 2025--2026 constraint-aware benchmarks.

\subsection{Foundations: Machine Learning for Network Telemetry}
The application of statistical machine learning to network intrusion detection originated with the realization that signature-based detection engines (such as Snort and Suricata) cannot detect novel, zero-day exploits or polymorphism \cite{sommer2010outside}. Early systems explored unsupervised anomaly detection and shallow classifiers (such as Support Vector Machines, Naive Bayes, and shallow Multi-Layer Perceptrons) trained on datasets like KDD Cup 1999 and NSL-KDD. However, these early models suffered from high false alarm rates, poor scalability, and severe feature representation artifacts \cite{sommer2010outside}.

The introduction of modern high-throughput flow extractors (such as CICFlowMeter \cite{sharafaldin2018toward}, Zeek, and YAF) coupled with the publication of realistic benchmark datasets (CSE-CIC-IDS2018 \cite{sharafaldin2018toward}, CIC-IDS2017, and UNSW-NB15 \cite{moustafa2015unsw}) shifted the community toward Gradient Boosted Decision Tree (GBDT) ensembles. Tree ensembles---specifically XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, and CatBoost \cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}---became the de facto gold standard for tabular network telemetry. Their popularity stems from several distinct structural advantages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Handling Mixed Tabular Types:} Flow records naturally blend continuous metrics (inter-arrival duration, byte rates) with discrete counts (packet totals, header lengths) and categorical attributes (TCP flags, protocols). Decision trees partition continuous and discrete variables natively without requiring complex embedding layers or continuous scaling.
    \item \textbf{Invariance to Monotonic Transformations:} Unlike deep neural networks, whose linear combinations are sensitive to feature scales and outliers, decision tree split points depend solely on the rank ordering of feature values.
    \item \textbf{Sub-Millisecond Inference Latency:} A trained tree ensemble can evaluate incoming flow records using simple nested conditional statements (branch comparisons), enabling wire-speed packet classification on standard commodity CPU and GPU hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase I: Naive Feature-Space Transfer (2016--2020)}
Following the discovery of adversarial examples in deep neural networks by Szegedy et al. and Goodfellow et al. \cite{goodfellow2014explaining}, a wave of initial studies investigated the vulnerability of machine learning-based NIDS to evasion attacks. 

Early works (such as those by Wang, Usama et al., and Pawlicki et al.) directly applied continuous gradient-based adversarial methods to tabular feature vectors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM):}
    \begin{equation}
        x_{\text{adv}} = x + \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}\left( \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(f(x), y) \right)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD):}
    \begin{equation}
        x^{(t+1)} = \Pi_{x + \mathcal{S}} \left( x^{(t)} + \alpha \cdot \text{sign}\left( \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(f(x^{(t)}), y) \right) \right)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Carlini-Wagner (C\&W) $L_2$ Optimization \cite{carlini2017towards}:}
    \begin{equation}
        \min_{\delta} \|\delta\|_2^2 + c \cdot \max\left( \max_{i \neq t} Z(x + \delta)_i - Z(x + \delta)_t, -\kappa \right)
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

These early studies reported extraordinary attack success rates, frequently exceeding $95\%$, leading to widespread claims that ML-based NIDS were fundamentally brittle. 

However, as subsequent security analyses revealed, these evaluations were fundamentally flawed. By optimizing perturbations $\delta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ inside continuous Euclidean space, the algorithms produced feature vectors that had no physical meaning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item They assigned fractional values to discrete count metrics (e.g., $23.47$ forward packets).
    \item They perturbed immutable attributes (e.g., changing Destination Port from 80 to 22.3).
    \item They produced negative inter-arrival times and negative durations.
    \item They violated physical conservation laws: forward payload byte counts were reduced below the minimum 20-byte TCP header size, or packet counts were increased while byte totals remained zero.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Phase II: The Problem-Space Paradigm Shift (2020--2024)}
In their landmark 2020 paper, \emph{``Intrusion Detection Meets Adversarial Machine Learning: Reality or Illusion?''}, Pierazzi et al. \cite{pierazzi2020intrusion} formalized the mathematical framework required to evaluate adversarial attacks in security domains. They demonstrated that an adversarial perturbation is an operational illusion unless it is mapped from the \textbf{Problem Space} $\mathcal{Z}$ to the \textbf{Feature Space} $\mathcal{X}$.

\subsubsection{The Pierazzi Framework}
Let $z \in \mathcal{Z}$ denote a real physical object (e.g., an executable binary, a PDF document, or a sequence of network packets). The feature extraction pipeline is a deterministic mapping:
\begin{equation}
    \phi: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{X}
\end{equation}
An adversary cannot manipulate feature vectors directly; they can only apply physical transformations $T \in \mathcal{T}$ in the problem space:
\begin{equation}
    z^* = T(z)
\end{equation}
A problem-space adversarial attack is formally defined as finding a transformation $T$ that satisfies three joint criteria:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classifier Evasion:}
    \begin{equation}
        f(\phi(T(z))) \neq y_{\text{true}}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Semantic Preservation:}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{S}(T(z)) = \mathcal{S}(z)
    \end{equation}
    meaning the malicious utility, exploit payload, or botnet command-and-control functionality must execute successfully on the target system.
    \item \textbf{Plausibility and Format Constraints:} $T(z)$ must adhere to valid format specifications (e.g., valid IP/TCP checksums, routable packet headers) so that intermediate networking devices (routers, firewalls, middleboxes) do not drop the transmission before it reaches the NIDS sensor.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Empirical Audits by Apruzzese et al.}
Following the formalization of Pierazzi et al., Apruzzese et al. \cite{apruzzese2022role, apruzzese2023modeling} conducted extensive empirical audits of over 50 published adversarial NIDS papers across top security venues. Their findings were devastating to the existing literature:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Over $96\%$ of published adversarial samples were physically un-routable.
    \item When feature-space adversarial perturbations were reconstructed into actual network packets and passed through standard middleboxes, the packets were either dropped due to corrupted protocol checksums or resulted in broken TCP handshakes.
    \item When evaluated against true packet parsers, the real attack success rate of conventional gradient attacks collapsed from $>90\%$ down to $0\%$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Tabular Constrained Benchmarks: CAA and TabularBench}
To address these limitations within tabular domains, Simonetto et al. \cite{simonetto2021constrained} introduced the \textbf{Constrained Adaptive Attack (CAA)}, which combined two complementary engines:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{CAPGD (Constrained Adaptive PGD):} A parameter-free gradient attack that projects continuous perturbations onto user-defined hyper-rectangular bounding boxes $[x_i^{\min}, x_i^{\max}]$ and enforces feature immutability.
    \item \textbf{MOEVA (Multi-Objective Evolutionary Adversarial Attack):} A genetic algorithm based on NSGA-II that optimizes a multi-objective fitness function penalizing both classifier detection confidence and constraint violation penalties.
\end{enumerate}

Subsequently, Sghamizi et al. \cite{sghamizi2024tabularbench} formalized these methods into \textbf{TabularBench}, establishing a standardized benchmark across deep learning and tree ensembles. While CAA represented significant progress over unconstrained PGD, it exhibited severe limitations in network telemetry:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It treated constraints as independent bounding boxes and linear penalties.
    \item It could not enforce non-linear coupled conservation laws ($\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Bytes}}{\text{Duration}}$), because perturbing one coordinate without immediately recomputing dependent coordinates violates physical laws at every intermediate optimization step.
    \item Its evolutionary component (MOEVA) required over 1,000 queries per sample, making it too slow for operational use.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Phase III: Tree Ensemble Solvers \& Formal Verification (2024--2026)}
Because Gradient Boosted Decision Trees are piecewise-constant, researchers sought exact mathematical representations of tree decision boundaries.

\subsubsection{Kantchelian's Exact MILP Evasion}
In their foundational paper, Kantchelian et al. \cite{kantchelian2016evasion} formulated adversarial evasion against tree ensembles as a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP). 

Given an ensemble of $T$ trees, where each tree $t$ has leaves $L_t$, a binary indicator variable $z_{t, l} \in \{0, 1\}$ is defined for each leaf node $l \in L_t$. Each leaf corresponds to a hyper-rectangular cell defined by split predicates:
\begin{equation}
    x_j \le \theta_{j} \quad \text{or} \quad x_j > \theta_{j}
\end{equation}
The exact minimal $L_1$ or $L_0$ perturbation is found by solving:
\begin{align}
    \min_{x^*, z} \quad & \|x^* - x_0\|_p \\
    \text{subject to} \quad & \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{l \in L_t} w_{t, l} z_{t, l} < \tau \\
    & \sum_{l \in L_t} z_{t, l} = 1, \quad \forall t \in \{1, \dots, T\} \\
    & z_{t, l} = 1 \implies x^* \in \text{Box}(l)
\end{align}

While mathematically exact, Kantchelian's MILP formulation suffers from severe practical limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Combinatorial Complexity:} The number of binary variables scales with the total number of leaves across all trees $\sum_{t=1}^T |L_t| \approx T \cdot 2^D$. For modern production ensembles with $T = 100$ trees and depth $D = 8$, the solver must reason over $25,600$ integer variables. The problem is NP-hard, and standard solvers (such as Gurobi or CPLEX) frequently timeout or exhaust system memory.
    \item \textbf{Linearity Restriction:} MILP solvers natively accept only linear constraints. When non-linear network flow invariants are introduced (e.g., $x_{\text{rate}} = \frac{x_{\text{bytes}}}{x_{\text{duration}}}$), the formulation becomes a Mixed-Integer Non-Linear Program (MINLP), which is generally intractable.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{SAT and SMT Formal Verification: XReason}
Between 2024 and 2026, researchers adapted Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) and Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) to formally verify and explain GBDT ensembles. Ignatiev et al. \cite{ignatiev2022formal} and tools such as \textbf{XReason} by Izza et al. \cite{xreason2024} encode tree ensemble splits into propositional Horn clauses. By querying modern SMT solvers (such as Z3), XReason can synthesize exact formal counterexamples and minimal explanations for XGBoost and LightGBM models.

However, formal SMT verification encounters the same fundamental barrier: reasoning over large ensembles requires massive memory allocations, and non-linear arithmetic constraints causes SMT solvers to enter non-terminating decision loops.

\subsection{Phase IV: Black-Box Heuristic Traffic Mutation (2021--2026)}
To circumvent mathematical feature-space abstractions entirely, security practitioners developed packet-level mutation engines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Traffic Manipulator (Han et al. \cite{han2021traffic}):} Uses Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to mutate raw PCAP files. It perturbs packet transmission intervals, injects dummy payload bytes, and introduces chaff packets.
    \item \textbf{Protocol-Aware GANs (Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2025protocol}):} Employs Auxiliary Classifier Generative Adversarial Networks (PA-ACGAN) to synthesize network telemetry complying with protocol headers.
    \item \textbf{LUCID (Doriguzzi-Corin et al. \cite{doriguzzi2024lucid}):} Evaluated lightweight convolutional detectors under adversarial traffic perturbations.
\end{itemize}

While these frameworks guarantee physical routability by operating directly on packets, their optimization engines treat the target classifier as an opaque black box. Because Genetic Algorithms and PSO randomly mutate parameters without knowing which tree splits are active, they require $1,000$ to $10,000$ queries per flow, taking several minutes per sample.

\subsection{The Critical Unresolved Gap}
As evidenced by Table~\ref{tab:sota_taxonomy_expanded}, the existing literature leaves a profound gap in the scientific landscape:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Methods that exploit mathematical structure (MILP, SMT, PGD) operate in unconstrained feature space, generating non-routable flows with 0\% physical feasibility.
    \item Methods that preserve physical feasibility (Traffic Manipulator, PA-ACGAN, MOEVA) treat the classifier as a black box, resulting in catastrophic query complexities ($10^3 - 10^4$ queries) and minute-scale latencies.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Flow-Field Evasion (FFE)} resolves this dilemma. By deriving the exact infinitesimal tangent vector fields directly from elementary physical packet operations, FFE conducts gradient-like directed search on tree decision scores while remaining mathematically confined to the physical flow manifold $\mathcal{M}_{\text{flow}}$ at every step.
