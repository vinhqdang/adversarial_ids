\subsection{Ablation Studies \& Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:ablation}

To systematically dissect the mechanics of Flow-Field Evasion, this subsection presents five extensive ablation studies. We evaluate: (i) the effect of the optimization step budget $M$; (ii) individual and combinatorial generator contributions; (iii) sensitivity to operational transmission costs $\vec{c}$; (iv) decision boundary facet snapping tolerances $\epsilon$; and (v) cross-architecture model transferability. Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_studies} synthesizes the empirical sensitivity dynamics across these five dimensions.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_ablations.pdf}

(a) Evasion convergence and $L_1$ operational cost scaling as a function of maximum step budget $M$, saturating at $M=20$. (b) Generator contribution ablation demonstrating that while temporal dilation alone achieves 94\% ASR, the full Lie cone is required for 100\% evasion. (c) Boundary snapping numerical tolerance $\epsilon$ versus boundary jitter and ASR, confirming complete stability at $\epsilon=10^{-4}$. (d) Cross-architecture adversarial transferability matrix (\%) demonstrating high black-box transferability ($\ge 91\%$) across unseen tree ensemble architectures.}
\label{fig:ablation_studies}

\paragraph{Key Insights on Step Budget.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rapid Early Convergence:} Even under an extreme budget of $M = 3$ steps, FFE achieves a $74.0\%$ evasion rate. At $M = 5$, ASR exceeds $90\%$.
    \item \textbf{Saturation at $M = 20$:} Increasing the budget from $M = 20$ to $M = 50$ yields no additional queries, because 100\% of flows converge within 15 steps.
    \item \textbf{Monotonic Cost Scaling:} As budget increases, the average $L_1$ perturbation cost rises smoothly and stabilizes, reflecting the distance required to reach the benign decision boundary.
\end{itemize}

To quantify the role played by each elementary network transformation, we evaluate FFE when individual generators or pairs of generators are selectively disabled. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_generators} reports the results.

\paragraph{Key Insights on Generator Mechanics.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Primariness of Temporal Dilation ($\vec{v}_{\text{time}}$):} Acting alone, temporal dilation achieves $94.0\%$ ASR. Because decision trees split aggressively on flow rate features ($x_8, x_9$), extending duration serves as a powerful universal attenuator of volumetric attack signatures.
    \item \textbf{Necessity of Full Cone for 100\% Evasion:} Temporal dilation alone fails on the remaining $6\%$ of attacks (primarily brute force credential attacks and stealthy infiltration beacons whose durations are already large). To evade these attacks, the optimizer must engage payload padding and window morphing.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Feasibility Failure Avoided:} Regardless of which subset of generators is active, the Physical Feasibility Rate (PFR) remains strictly $100.0\%$, verifying the robustness of Theorem~\ref{thm:invariant_universal}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Ablation Study 3: Sensitivity to Physical Operational Costs}
In Algorithm~\ref{alg:ffe_master}, the cost vector $\vec{c} = [c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4]^T$ determines the efficiency penalty $(-\nabla_k) / c_k$. We evaluate four distinct operational cost regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regime A (Default / Balanced):} $c_1 = 10^{-4}$ (delay is cheap), $c_2 = 10^{-2}$ (padding is moderate), $c_3 = 1.0$ (chaff packets cost socket overhead), $c_4 = 10^{-3}$ (window is cheap).
    \item \textbf{Regime B (Latency-Sensitive):} $c_1 = 10.0$ (delay is penalized heavily, e.g., in real-time gaming or high-frequency trading), $c_2 = 10^{-3}, c_3 = 0.1, c_4 = 10^{-3}$.
    \item \textbf{Regime C (Bandwidth-Constrained):} $c_2 = 10.0, c_3 = 10.0$ (data caps prevent adding dummy bytes or packets), $c_1 = 10^{-4}, c_4 = 10^{-4}$.
    \item \textbf{Regime D (Uniform / Unweighted):} $c_1 = c_2 = c_3 = c_4 = 1.0$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Ablation 3: Sensitivity to Operational Cost Regimes}\label{tab:ablation_costs}%
\begin{tabular*}{\columnwidth}{@{\extracolsep\fill}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Cost Regime} & \textbf{ASR (\%)} & \textbf{Avg Queries} & \textbf{Dominant Generator} & \textbf{Mean Delay Added} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Regime A (Default)}           & 100.0\% & 9.1  & $\vec{v}_{\text{time}}$ ($68\%$) & $142.5$ ms \\
\textbf{Regime B (Latency-Sensitive)}  & 96.0\%  & 11.4 & $\vec{v}_{\text{pad}}$ ($54\%$)  & $8.2$ ms \\
\textbf{Regime C (Bandwidth-Constrained)} & 98.0\% & 8.6 & $\vec{v}_{\text{time}}$ ($89\%$) & $210.4$ ms \\
\textbf{Regime D (Uniform / Flat)}     & 100.0\% & 9.4  & $\vec{v}_{\text{time}}$ ($62\%$) & $138.1$ ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular*}
\end{table}

Under Regime B (where transmission delays are penalized by 5 orders of magnitude), FFE automatically shifts its operational strategy: it de-prioritizes temporal dilation and instead relies on payload padding and chaff packet insertion, achieving $96.0\%$ evasion while reducing the average added delay from $142.5$ ms down to just $8.2$ ms.

\subsubsection{Ablation Study 4: Boundary Snapping Tolerance $\epsilon$}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:tsbs} (TSBS) snaps mutable features to active split thresholds: $x_j^* = \theta_j + \epsilon$. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_epsilon} evaluates the effect of the numerical offset $\epsilon \in \{0.0, 10^{-6}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-2}, 1.0\}$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Ablation 4: Boundary Snapping Numerical Offset $\epsilon$}\label{tab:ablation_epsilon}%
\begin{tabular*}{\columnwidth}{@{\extracolsep\fill}ccccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Offset $\epsilon$} & \textbf{ASR (\%)} & \textbf{PFR (\%)} & \textbf{Boundary Jitter (\%)} & \textbf{Avg $L_1$ Cost} \\
\midrule
$0.0$      & 92.0\%  & 100.0\% & 8.0\% (Floating-Point Ambiguity) & 28,680.1 \\
$10^{-6}$  & 99.0\%  & 100.0\% & 1.0\% (Precision Edge Case)      & 28,681.2 \\
$10^{-4}$  & 100.0\% & 100.0\% & 0.0\% (Completely Stable)        & 28,681.9 \\
$10^{-2}$  & 100.0\% & 100.0\% & 0.0\% (Completely Stable)        & 28,685.4 \\
$1.0$      & 100.0\% & 100.0\% & 0.0\% (Over-Perturbed)           & 28,710.2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular*}
\end{table}

When $\epsilon = 0.0$, numerical floating-point inaccuracies cause $8\%$ of samples lying exactly on the decision boundary $\theta_j$ to fail threshold comparisons in different compiler floating-point units. Setting $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$ provides robust, numerically stable facet traversal with negligible cost overhead.

\subsubsection{Ablation Study 5: Cross-Architecture Model Transferability}
In many operational settings, an attacker may not have direct query access to the deployed production NIDS. We evaluate the \textbf{black-box transferability} of adversarial flows generated by FFE: flows are crafted on a source surrogate model (e.g., XGBoost) and evaluated against unseen target architectures (LightGBM, CatBoost, and Random Forest). \paragraph{Cross-Model Analysis.}
Remarkably, FFE exhibits \textbf{exceptional cross-model transferability}: adversarial flows crafted against XGBoost achieve a \textbf{98.0\% ASR on LightGBM}, \textbf{95.0\% on CatBoost}, and \textbf{94.0\% on Random Forest}.

This high transferability occurs because FFE does not overfit to idiosyncratic decision split thresholds of a single model; instead, it steers the flow along physical vector fields that align with the true underlying benign data distribution. Because all tree architectures learn similar volumetric shortcuts from the benchmark dataset, moving a flow toward the physical manifold of benign web browsing successfully evades all tree models simultaneously.
