\subsection{Retraining variance, ablations and transferability}
\label{sec:ablation}

\paragraph{Retraining variance.} The single-seed benchmark cannot show how much of the ranking is luck of the split and the surrogate. We therefore repeated the whole pipeline for five seeds (new split, new XGBoost model, new surrogate and generator, new cohort of 300 detected flows) for FFE, CAPGD and the NIDSGAN-style generator (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}). FFE is at least as successful as CAPGD in all 15 runs and strictly better in 14 (the exception is a tie at 99.3\% on one CSE-CIC-IDS2018 seed). The gap on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 shrinks from 11.0 points on the main seed to 2.0 points on average (99.7\% against 97.7\%). On CIC-IDS2017 FFE also varies less (standard deviation 1.5 points against 11.9 for CAPGD and 15.7 for the generator), possibly because the surrogate attacks depend on the surrogate fitted in that run; on UNSW-NB15 FFE varies more than CAPGD (4.3 against 2.8).

\paragraph{Role of tree-split boundary snapping.} Switching TSBS off (Supplementary Table~S2) leaves Real ASR unchanged on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (99.5\% in both cases) and lowers it from 99.2\% to 97.5\% on CIC-IDS2017 and from 57.6\% to 54.6\% on UNSW-NB15, while the mean number of queries rises from 11.6, 13.5 and 51.5 to 16.5, 14.6 and 93.7 with TSBS. Snapping therefore rescues at most three more points of Real ASR and costs up to 1.8 times as many queries. TSBS therefore accounts for little of FFE's success, and we do not claim that boundary snapping makes the attack query-efficient; we did not isolate the benefit of the Lie structure from a greedy search over the same four moves.

\paragraph{Other ablations.} Step-budget, generator, cost-regime and snapping-offset ablations are in Supplementary Section~S4: success rises from 42.8\% at $M=1$ to 98.9\% at $M\ge 10$, no single generator suffices (10.2\%--66.1\%), and the cost regime and offset are uninformative on this saturated corpus.

\paragraph{Transferability.} We crafted FFE flows against one architecture and scored them with the other three, using the 221 malicious flows that all four detect when clean, so that a transfer is not the target's own false negative; all four models share the training partition. Flows crafted against XGBoost evade LightGBM in 71.5\% of the cases and Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001random} in 55.7\%; those crafted against LightGBM, CatBoost and Random Forest evade the non-CatBoost targets in 63.8\%--78.7\%. CatBoost is the hardest target (24.0\% for the XGBoost, LightGBM and Random Forest sources), although attacks against CatBoost itself succeed in only 69.7\% of the cases (Supplementary Table~S4).
