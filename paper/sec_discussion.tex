\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{What the Results Support, and What They Do Not}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Feasibility.} Every flow FFE produced passes the schema check, so ``feasible'' means feasible with respect to the 12-feature schema. We replayed the first 300 attacked flows per corpus (schema-level success 99.3\%, 99.3\% and 59.7\%) as synthetic packet traces and re-extracted the features (Supplementary Section~S8): 99.3\%--100\% of the evasions persist when chaff packets carry the booked bytes, but only 68.2\% on UNSW-NB15 if chaff packets are empty, and no real capture or network stack was involved. A deployed detector may use features outside the twelve (inter-arrival statistics, flag counts) that the four actions also change.

\paragraph{Comparison with the baselines.} FFE exceeds each of the seven baselines on each corpus, significantly so (Table~\ref{tab:master_evasion_benchmark}), but the ranking is not dominance: a white-box leaf-tuple-style attack that projects every candidate equals it on CSE-CIC-IDS2018, evades slightly more on CIC-IDS2017 and reaches 90.2\% against 57.6\% on UNSW-NB15 (Supplementary Section~S7), so the four generators limit FFE's reach there. It needs 2.8--20 times fewer queries than PSO and MOEVA on the same flows, and its score-only variant without TSBS, which needs no thresholds, still beats every baseline (99.5\%, 97.5\% and 54.6\%; PSO and MOEVA ran on 200 flows). Surrogate-based attacks need far fewer target queries (CAPGD 3.2--16.2 against 14.6--93.7) and come within 10--11 points on the two easier corpora, but fall to 21.1\% on UNSW-NB15, where the surrogate agrees least with the target (87.6\%), which may explain it. The cost advantage is partly by construction, and in the normalized $L_1$ distance FFE is not smaller than PSO on two corpora. Boundary snapping adds at most three points at up to 1.8 times the queries (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), so it accounts for little of FFE's success; we did not isolate the Lie structure from a plain greedy search over the same four moves.

\paragraph{Defenses.} Lie-guided adversarial training nearly halves FFE's success at its training budget but protects far less against a larger budget or another attack, and no model brings evasion below 46\% (Section~\ref{sec:defense}); the defenses were evaluated on one corpus and one seed, and the masked-feature defense implements the infinite-penalty limit.

\paragraph{Limits of the method.} TSBS needs the split thresholds (Tier~1); without it FFE needs only the score of the target. An attacker who cannot query the target at all depends on transfer, and flows crafted against one architecture evade the others in 24\%--79\% of the cases on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (Supplementary Section~S5), with CatBoost the hardest target. The packet realization has not been tested on a real network; control is restricted to forward traffic, deep packet inspection is out of scope, and rate dilution lowers the intensity of volumetric floods.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} The main comparison uses one training seed; the five-seed repetition covers three attacks. Cohorts contain only flows that the models detect when clean (77.6\% of the CSE-CIC-IDS2018 test attacks). MOEVA and PSO ran on 200 flows. CAPGD and the NIDSGAN-style generator are our re-implementations with one small surrogate, the baselines use default budgets, and the white-box control is our own greedy re-implementation, not LT-Attack or Cascioli et al., run on one seed. The feasibility check has a 5\% tolerance on the coupled rates for all methods. The random split lets near-duplicates fall on both sides, so accuracies are optimistic.

Each generator has a protocol-compliant packet realization: sender pacing for temporal dilation, padding in protocol fields or ignored application frames (\texttt{SSH\_MSG\_IGNORE}) \cite{rfc4253}, payload-free pure ACK or keep-alive chaff \cite{rfc9293,rfc7323} or zero-length UDP datagrams \cite{rfc768}, and handshake window morphing, with sequence numbers and checksums updated via proxy \cite{rfc791,rfc9293,rfc768}.

\subsection{Concluding Remarks and Future Directions}
\label{sec:concluding_remarks}

This article has measured, at full scale, how vulnerable tree-ensemble NIDS with high benchmark accuracy are to evasion that respects a flow schema. Flow-Field Evasion models four packet-level actions as infinitesimal generators on the flow manifold; trajectories in the continuous Lie cone preserve the conservation invariants and a projection enforces them on the discrete schema. On the full, deduplicated CSE-CIC-IDS2018, CIC-IDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 captures, FFE evades XGBoost in 99.5\%, 99.2\% and 57.6\% of the detected attacks it targets, with schema-feasible flows and 14.6--93.7 target queries per flow. It is significantly more successful than all seven baselines and stays ahead of CAPGD over five retrained seeds, but a white-box tree attack that projects every candidate matches or exceeds it. Evasion depends on the target: LightGBM on CIC-IDS2017 resists FFE because the window target lies in an empty region of the training data (we did not examine the residual failures at an in-support target), and CatBoost resists transfer. Lie-guided adversarial training halves FFE success at the budget it was trained on but protects much less against stronger attacks. Future work includes replay on a real network stack, the official white-box tree attacks, defenses evaluated on all corpora, several seeds and attacks other than the one used for augmentation, and the remaining public NIDS corpora.
